# Everyday Legal Concepts

## What makes a contract binding

A binding contract generally needs: an offer, acceptance of that offer, consideration (each side gives something of value), mutual intent to be bound, and legal capacity of the parties. Most contracts don't need to be in writing to be valid — but some categories do under the "statute of frauds" (commonly: real estate transfers, agreements that can't be performed within a year, and guarantees of another's debt). Written contracts matter mainly as proof of what was agreed.

## Breach of contract

A breach happens when one party fails to perform as promised. Remedies typically include damages (money to put the injured party where they'd be if the contract were performed), and occasionally specific performance (a court order to actually do the thing, mostly for unique goods or real estate). The injured party usually has a duty to mitigate — take reasonable steps to reduce their losses.

## Security deposits (tenancy)

Most jurisdictions require landlords to return a security deposit within a set window after move-out (commonly 14–30 days), minus itemized deductions for damage beyond normal wear and tear. Unpaid rent can usually be deducted; routine repainting and carpet wear usually cannot. If a landlord misses the deadline or fails to itemize, many jurisdictions allow the tenant to recover a multiple of the deposit. Document the unit's condition with photos at move-in and move-out.

## Eviction basics

Evictions follow a formal court process: written notice first (e.g. notice to pay or vacate), then a court filing, a hearing, and only then removal by an official. "Self-help" evictions — changing locks, cutting utilities, removing belongings — are illegal nearly everywhere, regardless of unpaid rent. Timelines and notice requirements vary heavily by jurisdiction.

## Small claims court

Small claims courts resolve modest money disputes (limits vary, commonly $5,000–$20,000) with simplified procedures and no requirement to hire a lawyer. Typical flow: send a demand letter, file a petition and pay a small fee, serve the defendant, present documents and photos at a short hearing, and collect on the judgment if you win. Collection is the hard part — a judgment is not automatic payment.

## Employee vs independent contractor

The label in the contract doesn't decide it — courts and agencies look at control and independence: who sets hours and methods, whose tools are used, whether the work is core to the business, financial risk, and permanence. Misclassification matters because employees get minimum wage, overtime, unemployment insurance, and payroll tax withholding; contractors don't. Tests differ by agency and state (IRS common-law test, ABC test in some states).

## At-will employment

In most US states, employment is "at-will": either side can end it at any time, for any reason or no reason — except an illegal one. Illegal reasons include discrimination based on protected characteristics (race, sex, religion, age 40+, disability, etc.), retaliation for protected activity (reporting harassment, whistleblowing, taking family/medical leave), and reasons violating an employment contract.

## Statutes of limitations

Every claim type has a filing deadline that varies by jurisdiction and claim: commonly 2–4 years for personal injury, 3–6 years for written contracts, shorter for claims against government entities (sometimes with notice requirements measured in months). Missing the deadline usually kills the claim entirely — deadlines are one of the strongest reasons to consult a lawyer early.

## Power of attorney and basic estate documents

- **Will** — directs who receives your property and who cares for minor children; goes through probate.
- **Power of attorney** — authorizes someone to act for you in financial matters; "durable" versions survive your incapacity.
- **Healthcare directive / medical POA** — states treatment wishes and names a medical decision-maker.
- **Beneficiary designations** on retirement accounts and life insurance override the will — keep them current.

## When to hire a lawyer

Strong signals: any deadline or court paper you've received, potential liability above what you can afford to lose, disputes over real estate, family law matters (custody, divorce), criminal charges of any kind, and contracts with significant money or long commitments. Many attorneys offer free or low-cost initial consultations; legal aid organizations and state bar referral services help when cost is a barrier.
