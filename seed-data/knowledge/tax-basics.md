# US Federal Income Tax Basics

## Gross income vs taxable income

Gross income is everything you earn (wages, interest, business income, capital gains). Taxable income is what's left after subtracting adjustments ("above-the-line" deductions like traditional IRA or HSA contributions) and either the standard deduction or itemized deductions. Tax brackets apply to taxable income, not gross income.

## Deduction vs credit

A deduction reduces your taxable income, so its value depends on your marginal rate — a $1,000 deduction saves $220 for someone in the 22% bracket. A credit reduces your tax bill dollar-for-dollar — a $1,000 credit saves $1,000 regardless of bracket. Credits are more valuable than deductions of the same size. Some credits (like part of the Child Tax Credit and the Earned Income Tax Credit) are refundable, meaning they can produce a refund even if you owe no tax.

## Standard deduction vs itemizing

You take whichever is larger. The standard deduction is a fixed amount based on filing status (for 2025: $15,000 single, $30,000 married filing jointly, $22,500 head of household — sample figures). Itemizing adds up specific expenses: state and local taxes (capped at $10,000), mortgage interest, charitable donations, and large medical expenses. Most filers take the standard deduction; itemizing usually only wins for homeowners with large mortgages or people with major charitable giving.

## Marginal vs effective rate

The US system is progressive: each bracket rate applies only to the income within that bracket. Moving into a higher bracket does NOT raise the tax on all your income — only on the dollars above the threshold. Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar; your effective rate is total tax divided by taxable income, and it is always lower than your marginal rate.

## Filing statuses

- **Single** — unmarried as of December 31.
- **Married filing jointly** — most married couples; usually the lowest total tax.
- **Married filing separately** — occasionally useful (income-driven student loan plans, separating liability), but loses several credits.
- **Head of household** — unmarried, paid more than half the cost of keeping a home, and have a qualifying dependent. Better brackets and standard deduction than single.

## Key deadlines

- **April 15** — individual returns (Form 1040) and payment due; also the deadline for prior-year IRA/HSA contributions.
- **October 15** — extended filing deadline if you filed Form 4868. An extension extends time to FILE, not time to PAY — interest and penalties accrue on unpaid balances after April 15.
- **Quarterly estimated taxes** (self-employed and others without withholding): due roughly April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year.

## Withholding and refunds

A refund means you overpaid during the year via withholding — it's your own money returned, not a bonus. If you owe a large balance or get a very large refund, adjust Form W-4 with your employer. Underpaying substantially during the year can trigger an underpayment penalty; the safe harbor is paying at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% for higher incomes).

## Common adjustments and savings vehicles

- **Traditional 401(k)/IRA** — contributions reduce taxable income now; withdrawals taxed in retirement.
- **Roth 401(k)/IRA** — no deduction now; qualified withdrawals tax-free later.
- **HSA** (requires a high-deductible health plan) — deductible going in, grows tax-free, tax-free for medical expenses; the only triple-advantaged account.
- **Capital gains** — assets held over one year get preferential long-term rates (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income); short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income.
